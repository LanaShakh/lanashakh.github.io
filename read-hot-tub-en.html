A cabin with a hot tub: how furako differs from a metal bathing vat, and what to ask before booking

Published original · RUBack to portfolio

A cabin with a hot tub brings something to a countryside break that a hotel or an ordinary holiday cottage rarely offers: warm water under an open sky and time to unwind. It is a pleasure in its own right, and even better after a kayaking trip or a day on quad bikes.

There is more choice than you might expect. Hot tubs differ in size, heating and location, and those details shape the whole stay. This guide explains what to look for and what to ask the property before booking. Our experience comes from Na Krayu Zemli, a glamping retreat near Kozelsk, where every chalet has its own furako hot tub.

What is a furako hot tub?

Furako comes from Japan: a large wooden tub filled with hot water, deep enough to sit immersed up to your shoulders. What happens next is up to you. Sink into the warmth until you have no wish to leave, watch the sunset from the water, take photographs with steam rising around you, or warm up after a long walk.

Our tubs are made of larch. Water helps this wood become stronger over time, so the tubs stay outdoors throughout the year. Bathing in a wooden tub has an imperial heritage: in old Japan, furako made from valuable timber were reserved for nobles and members of the imperial family. Japanese writings also noted that a hot bath is at its best in cold weather. In the Kaluga region, we have found that true as well.

How furako differs from a metal bathing vat

A metal bathing vat makes its character clear: a steel vessel, a fire underneath, smoke rising into the air. It has the appeal of an outdoor camp, but the fire burns directly below you and needs attention throughout the evening.

Furako offers a different experience. Warm wood instead of steel, evenly heated water rather than alternating hot and cool layers, and the scent of larch above the steam. The stove quietly does its job. You can rest your head on the broad rim, watch the first stars appear above the valley, and decide whether to have a drink now or later.

What we add to the water

You can choose the mood of the evening in advance. Our cedar option brings the scent of a deep forest, rising with the steam. For a winter feeling, the mulled-wine-inspired option combines citrus and spices with hot water, like the atmosphere of a Christmas market. From autumn to spring, we also offer a milk bath: silky water that might make you think of Cleopatra and the stories told about her baths.

Tell us which option you would like when booking, and we will have everything ready for your arrival. Pine branches are used for the cedar option; flowers and citrus can be added for a special occasion.

What to look for when choosing a cabin with a hot tub

Every listing has attractive photographs. The details that determine your evening are often harder to find. Check these points before you book.

Is the tub private or shared?

At larger holiday complexes, one tub may serve the entire property and be rented by the hour. That can work well, but the water is prepared afresh for each group and takes several hours, so you need to book a slot in advance. With a private tub on your terrace, the water is prepared for you and the evening is yours.

What can you see from the water — and who can see you?

Listings rarely describe the view from inside the tub, although it can make more difference than many other details. Looking at a forest and looking at a car park are two very different experiences. Check how close the neighbouring cabins are, too: few people want to bathe in full view of other guests. Ask for a photograph taken from the tub itself, rather than a photograph of the tub from outside.

When will the water be hot?

A familiar disappointment is arriving in the evening and waiting until late at night because the fire was started too late or the wood was damp. This matters especially for a one-night stay: a few hours of waiting can take up a large part of your break. Ask directly when heating will start and when the water will be ready.

Does it really work in winter?

“Available in winter” should mean more than a tub standing in the yard. The water needs to reach the right temperature even in freezing weather, and both the tub and the path to it should be cleared of snow. Cold-weather bathing creates the thick cloud of steam seen in photographs, but only at properties that take winter preparation seriously.

How many people can actually fit?

Almost every tub looks spacious in a photograph. Ask about its capacity in people. Four guests sharing a two-person tub may spend the evening taking turns beside the water.

These five questions take about ten minutes of correspondence and can save a weekend from unpleasant surprises. Pay attention to the answers as well. A hot tub is one of the more demanding parts of running a countryside property: it needs firewood, maintenance and daily attention. A well-kept tub and specific answers often suggest the rest of the property receives the same care.

What to ask before booking

Once you have chosen a property, one short message to the manager can clarify the practical details:

  1. Is the hot tub included in the accommodation price or charged separately?
  2. When will the water be ready on arrival day?
  3. Is the water changed between guests, and how is it maintained?
  4. Can children use the tub?
  5. Is there a shower nearby for rinsing before and afterwards?
  6. Will someone show you how to use it at check-in? Five minutes with a member of staff can save an evening of guessing which controls to touch.

It is a good sign when the property gives specific answers, including figures, without trying to talk you into a booking. Vague replies often mean the tub is more of a feature in a photograph than a working part of your stay.

Frequently asked questions

What is a furako hot tub?

A furako is a large Japanese-style wooden tub for bathing outdoors in hot water. You sit immersed up to your shoulders in water at 37–40°C. Our tubs are made of larch and stand on the terrace of every chalet.

How does furako differ from a sauna?

A sauna warms you with hot air, approaching 80°C, with short visits to the steam room. A furako warms you with water at a comfortable temperature, outdoors. Many people combine the two, starting with the sauna and then moving to the tub.

How does a metal bathing vat differ from furako?

A metal vat is heated by a fire directly underneath. Furako is a wooden tub heated by a stove beside it or behind a partition. With a metal vat, the fire needs attention while you bathe. With furako at our retreat, the water is prepared before you arrive.

Can you use a hot tub in winter?

Yes. Winter is considered one of the best seasons: thick steam rises from the water, with snow all around. Ask the property whether it heats the water in freezing conditions and clears snow from the tub. Ours operate all year.

What is added to the water?

Herbs, pine branches and aromatic bath preparations can be added. We offer three options: cedar, mulled-wine-inspired citrus and spices, and a milk bath available from autumn to spring. Choose yours when booking.

Can children use the tub?

It depends on the property and the child’s age, so ask before booking. At our retreat, children use the tubs with adults. It is best to discuss the details for your child when arranging your stay.

Is there a time limit for using furako?

Water at a comfortable temperature makes this a different experience from short sessions in a steam room. At our retreat, there is no booking time limit on tub use. It is heated for check-in, there is spare firewood on the terrace, and additional heating is free.
